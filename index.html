§ 1 · Vectors

Arrows, equivalence classes, and the things they shadow.

A vector is the geometric object. Its components are the shadow that object casts in a chosen basis. Tensor analysis is the discipline of writing equations that respect the difference.

rank (1,0) upper index $v^i$ contravariant
Definition
Free vector

In flat $\mathbb{R}^n$, a vector is an equivalence class of directed line segments $(p, q)$ under the relation

$(p_1, q_1) \sim (p_2, q_2) \iff q_1 - p_1 = q_2 - p_2.$

Two arrows are equivalent if one is a parallel translate of the other. The vector $\vec v$ is the entire equivalence class.

This definition works in flat space. On curved spaces — the sphere, spacetime — parallel translation is path-dependent, so we attach a separate tangent space $T_p M$ to each point. Vectors at distinct points live in distinct spaces.

Components

Pick a basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$. Every vector has a unique decomposition $\vec v = v^i e_i$ (Einstein convention: sum over $i$). The upper index marks contravariant character.

A vector is not its components. The components are coordinate-dependent labels; the vector is the geometric object that exists independently of the basis you happened to pick.

§ 2 · Linear algebra refresher

What a basis is, and how to know you have one.

Everything in tensor analysis assumes a working memory of basis, linear independence, change-of-basis matrices, and inverses.

Essentials

Vector space $V$: a set you can add and scale, obeying the usual rules.

Basis $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$: every $v \in V$ has a unique decomposition $v = v^i e_i$.

Linear independence: $a^i e_i = 0$ forces every $a^i = 0$.

Linear map $L : V \to W$: $L(au + bv) = a\,L(u) + b\,L(v)$.

The basis test

$n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space form a basis if and only if the matrix of their columns has nonzero determinant.

Worked examples

$\{(1,3),(2,6)\}$: $\det = 0$. Not a basis.   $\{(1,0),(1,1)\}$: $\det = 1$. Basis.   $\{(1,2),(3,4),(5,6)\}$: three vectors in 2D — cannot be a basis.

Inverse of a $2\times 2$ matrix

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, \quad M^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}.$$

The determinant $ad - bc$ is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns. Zero area: no inverse.

Change-of-basis matrix
$M^i{}_j$ (also: transition matrix, Jacobian, Lorentz transformation)

$\tilde e_j = M^i{}_j \, e_i.$ The columns of $M$ are the new basis vectors in the old basis. Because both are bases, $M$ is invertible.

§ 3 · Contravariant transformation

How vector components transform.

The single derivation that the rest of tensor analysis depends on.

Write $v$ in two bases: $v = v^i e_i = \tilde v^j \tilde e_j.$ Substitute $\tilde e_j = M^i{}_j e_i$, collect by $e_i$, use uniqueness:

$$v^i = M^i{}_j \, \tilde v^j.$$

Apply $M^{-1}$:

Transformation law · contravariant

$$\boxed{\;\tilde v^j = (M^{-1})^j{}_i \, v^i\;}$$

Components transform with $M^{-1}$, opposite to the basis. Contravariant. Upper index.

Concrete check (doubled basis)

$\tilde e_i = 2 e_i$: $M = 2I$, $M^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{2}I$. Old components $(4,6)$ → new components $(2,3)$. Basis doubled, components halved. Same arrow, fewer ruler-lengths.

Components and basis are inversely yoked. Double the rulers, halve the readings. That is contravariance.

§ 4 · Covectors

Linear measurements, stacks of surfaces, the lower index.

The dual of a vector space. Elements are not arrows but rulers: they take vectors and return numbers.

rank (0,1) lower index $\omega_i$ covariant
Definition
Covector (dual vector, one-form)

A linear map $\omega : V \to \mathbb{R}$. Components $\omega_i := \omega(e_i)$. For any $v = v^j e_j$: $\omega(v) = \omega_j v^j$ — invariant scalar.

Transformation law · covariant

$$\boxed{\;\tilde \omega_j = M^i{}_j \, \omega_i\;}$$

Covector components transform with $M$, same direction as the basis. Covariant. Lower index.

Geometrically: a vector is an arrow. A covector is a stack of parallel hyperplanes. $\omega(v)$ counts how many the arrow pierces.

A vector points. A covector measures. The pairing $\omega_i v^i$ — one upper, one lower — is coordinate-invariant.
Canonical example · the gradient

$\partial f/\partial x^i$ is a covector. The directional derivative $v^i \partial f/\partial x^i$ is a covariant–contravariant contraction. You have been doing this since multivariable calculus.

§ 5 · The metric tensor

Where length, angle, and signature come from.

The metric pairs two vectors to produce a number. Lengths, angles, raising and lowering of indices, and the gravitational field in GR all live in $g_{ij}$.

rank (0,2) symmetric nondegenerate
Definition
Metric tensor $g$

A map $g : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ that is bilinear, symmetric ($g(u,v) = g(v,u)$), and nondegenerate ($g(u,v)=0\;\forall v \Rightarrow u=0$).

Components: $g_{ij} := g(e_i, e_j)$. For any $u, v$: $g(u,v) = g_{ij} u^i v^j$.

What the components mean

  • $\|v\|^2 = g_{ij} v^i v^j$. In 2D: $g_{11}(v^1)^2 + 2g_{12}v^1v^2 + g_{22}(v^2)^2$. The factor of 2 is not a typo.
  • Diagonal $g_{ii}$ = squared length of $e_i$. Off-diagonal $g_{ij}$ = $\|e_i\|\|e_j\|\cos\theta_{ij}$: basis skew.
  • $e_1 \perp e_2 \iff g_{12} = 0$.
Off-diagonal entries contribute. Always expand the full double sum $g_{ij}v^iv^j$ — all four terms in 2D, all nine in 3D.

Examples

Euclidean: $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$. Minkowski (SR): $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$. One minus sign: all of causality. Schwarzschild (GR): $g_{\mu\nu}$ depends on $r$ — that position-dependence is gravity.

Raising and lowering

$v_i := g_{ij} v^j$ (lower). $v^i := g^{ij} v_j$ (raise), where $g^{ij} g_{jk} = \delta^i{}_k$. In Cartesian Euclidean coordinates this changes nothing numerically. Everywhere else it does.

$\det g > 0$: Riemannian geometry. $\det g = 0$: degenerate, not a metric. $\det g < 0$ or mixed signs: Lorentzian — spacetime, light cones.

§ 6 · Tensor products and rank

Building higher-rank tensors from vectors and covectors.

A rank-$(p,q)$ tensor is a multilinear map eating $p$ covectors and $q$ vectors and returning a real number.

Definition
Rank-$(p, q)$ tensor

$T : \underbrace{V^* \times \cdots}_{p} \times \underbrace{V \times \cdots}_{q} \to \mathbb{R},$ multilinear. Components: $T^{i_1\cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}$. Dimension: $n^{p+q}$.

The cast, recategorized

  • Scalar = rank $(0,0)$.   Vector $v^i$ = rank $(1,0)$.   Covector $\omega_i$ = rank $(0,1)$.
  • Metric $g_{ij}$ = rank $(0,2)$, symmetric.   Inverse metric $g^{ij}$ = rank $(2,0)$.
  • Linear map $L^i{}_j$ = rank $(1,1)$. Every matrix is a tensor.
  • Stress, strain, electromagnetic field, Riemann curvature: higher-rank with specific symmetries.

Tensor product $\otimes$ and transformation law

$(v \otimes w)^{ij} := v^i w^j$. Product of ranks $(p_1,q_1)$ and $(p_2,q_2)$ gives rank $(p_1+p_2, q_1+q_2)$. Non-commutative: $v\otimes w \neq w\otimes v$.

Any rank-$(p,q)$ tensor decomposes: $T = T^{ij} e_i \otimes e_j$ (summed). The transformation law:

$$\tilde T^{i_1\cdots i_p}{}_{j_1\cdots j_q} = (M^{-1})^{i_1}{}_{k_1}\cdots M^{l_1}{}_{j_1}\cdots T^{k_1\cdots}{}_{l_1\cdots}.$$

Each upper index: factor of $M^{-1}$. Each lower index: factor of $M$.

§ 7 · Contraction

The rank-lowering operation that produces invariants.

Set one upper and one lower index equal and sum. Rank falls by $(1,1)$; invariance is preserved.

Definition
Contraction

For $T^i{}_j$: contraction is $T^i{}_i = \sum_i T^i{}_i$. A rank-$(p,q)$ tensor contracts to rank-$(p-1,q-1)$.

Why it is invariant

Transformation law: $\tilde T^i{}_i = (M^{-1})^i{}_k M^l{}_i T^k{}_l$. The shared index $i$ assembles $M M^{-1} = I$: $\delta^l{}_k T^k{}_l = T^k{}_k$. The basis-change matrices annihilate each other.

The trace of a matrix is basis-invariant because it is a tensor contraction. This is the same theorem.

Things that are secretly contractions

  • Pairing: $\omega_i v^i$.   Dot product: $g_{ij} u^i v^j$.   Trace: $L^i{}_i$.
  • Divergence: $\partial_i v^i$.   Laplacian: $g^{ij}\partial_i\partial_j f$.   Ricci scalar: $g^{ij}R_{ij}$.
  • Raising/lowering: $v_i = g_{ij}v^j$ and $v^i = g^{ij}v_j$ are each a product with the metric followed by contraction.

The whole algebra in three moves

Tensor product
Combine two tensors. Ranks add. Indices concatenate.
Contraction
Pair one upper with one lower, sum. Ranks decrease. Produces invariants.
Linear combination
Add same-rank tensors. Scale by scalars.

§ 8 · What comes next

From a point to a field, from algebra to geometry.

Everything above lives at a single point. Two directions from here.

Branch B · symmetries and applied tensors

Any rank-2 tensor splits into: a trace, a symmetric traceless part, and an antisymmetric part — volume change, shape change, rotation. Eigenvalues reappear as principal axes of stress and strain. Specific tensors: stress, strain, moment of inertia, electromagnetic field $F_{\mu\nu}$.

Branch A · manifolds and curvature

Tensor fields vary from point to point. Partial derivatives of tensors fail the transformation law — the basis varies with position. The covariant derivative fixes this via Christoffel symbols. Non-commuting covariant derivatives give the Riemann curvature tensor. Then geodesics, Ricci tensor, Einstein's field equations.

Both branches use exactly the algebra in this notebook.
